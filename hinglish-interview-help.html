Interview help in Hinglish — because your interviews aren't in one language

Real Indian interviews switch between English and Hindi mid-sentence. Tools built in America transcribe the English and garble the rest. Interview Sarthi follows the interviewer either way — and answers the way you'd actually speak.

The problem nobody built for

"Aapne is project mein caching kyun add kiya, and how did you decide the TTL?" — that's one sentence, two languages, and it's how interviews at Indian companies actually sound. An English-only transcriber turns the Hindi half into noise, so the AI answers the wrong question or answers in stiff formal English that sounds nothing like you. If you've tried a US interview copilot and found it useless the moment the interviewer relaxed into Hindi, this is why.

What answering in Hinglish looks like

Interviewer asks:

"Aapne Redis kyun use kiya us project mein?"

Interview Sarthi drafts, from your own resume:

"Honestly, latency ki wajah se. Session lookups har request pe ho rahe the, Postgres pe ~40 ms lag raha tha. Redis pe 1 ms ke andar — aur TTL se expiry free mein mil gayi."

Notice what that answer is not: it's not a translated paragraph, and it's not textbook Hindi. It's the code-mixed way engineers in India actually talk — technical words in English, connective tissue in Hindi — grounded in the Redis project from your CV, because generic essays are the fastest way to sound like you're reading.

When the interview is in English, so are the answers

The tool mirrors the interviewer. Formal English question → crisp English answer. Hindi/Hinglish question → Hinglish answer. You don't configure anything; it follows the conversation the way you would.

Who this helps most

Candidates strongest in Hindi who freeze composing English answers under pressure — the draft gives you the sentence structure, you deliver it your way. Engineers interviewing at Indian services and product companies, where HR rounds especially run bilingual. Anyone re-entering interviews after years — the vocabulary comes back when it's on screen in the mix of languages you think in.
