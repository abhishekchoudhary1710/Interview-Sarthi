Is it OK to use AI in a job interview?

What is clearly fine, what is clearly not, what is genuinely grey — and how this actually gets noticed, which is almost never the way people think.

Updated 29 August 2026 · 7 min read

This question gets answered badly in both directions. One camp says any AI in a hiring process is cheating, full stop. The other says everyone does it and the rules are theatre. Neither is a useful answer if you have an interview on Thursday.

Here is a more practical framing: the rules are usually written down somewhere, and the risk is not evenly distributed. Some uses are unambiguously fine and widely encouraged. Some will cost you the offer and, in the worst case, the job you already have. The middle is real, and it is worth knowing which part of it you are standing in.

Nothing here is legal advice. If you have signed something, the thing you signed governs — go read it.

Clearly fine

  • Preparing with AI. Generating likely questions, drafting and rewriting your own answers, researching the company, practising out loud against a bot. This is now standard advice from career services, and no employer objects.
  • Improving your resume and cover letter, as long as everything on it is true.
  • Mock interviews with an AI that asks and scores. Same category as practising with a friend.
  • Reviewing afterwards. Transcribing your own performance and looking at where you rambled is one of the highest-return things you can do, and involves nobody else's consent.
  • Using AI openly in a take-home when the instructions permit it — increasingly they do, because the job itself involves AI. Read the brief.

Clearly not fine

  • Proctored assessments. If a platform is watching your camera and screen, using anything is a violation you agreed to in writing when you clicked start.
  • Anywhere you signed or clicked a no-AI attestation. Many assessment platforms now put one on the first screen. That is a contract term, not a suggestion.
  • Live coding rounds where the interviewer said not to. They usually say it explicitly.
  • Claiming experience you do not have. This is the one that actually ends careers, and it is not really about AI — AI just makes it easier to do fluently. An answer that describes a system you never built is a false statement to an employer, whoever drafted it.
  • Anything at all in a government, defence, or regulated-sector selection process. The consequences there are legal, not professional.

The grey middle

Unstructured conversational interviews, where nothing was signed and nothing was said. Notes on a second screen. A prepared STAR outline you glance at. A live copilot drafting a reply while you talk.

Employers' actual positions here vary more than you would guess. Plenty of interviewers do not care whether you have notes open — they care whether you can hold a conversation about your own work at depth. Others consider any real-time assistance a misrepresentation of the thing they are trying to measure. You usually cannot tell which one you have.

The useful test is not “will I get caught”. It is: if the interviewer could see exactly what I was doing, would the offer survive? That question sorts the grey middle quickly. Glancing at a bullet list of your own achievements: survives. Reading out a generated answer about a project you did not work on: does not.

How this actually gets noticed

People assume detection means software catching them. Mostly it does not. In practice it is:

  • The follow-up question. This is the big one, and no tool solves it. You give a polished answer about sharding a database; they ask what your shard key was and why; the pause gives you away. Interviewers probe two or three levels down precisely because it separates lived experience from a good summary.
  • Rhythm. A consistent two-second pause before every answer, or eyes tracking left-to-right while “thinking”, reads as reading. Experienced interviewers notice this quickly on video.
  • Register mismatch. Written-sounding prose delivered out loud. People do not say “furthermore” in conversation.
  • Proctoring software, in the narrow set of cases where it is running.
  • The job itself. The slowest and most expensive way to find out, for both parties. Being hired into a role you cannot do is not a win.

What it costs if it goes wrong

Worth being concrete, because people weigh this vaguely. An offer withdrawn is the common outcome. Being blacklisted from a company's ATS — and sometimes its whole hiring group — happens. Termination later for misrepresentation is rarer but real, and it is worse than not getting the job, because you have already left the last one.

Against that: the upside of assistance in a single round is usually marginal. This is a bad risk-reward trade in the settings where the rules are explicit, which is why the first two sections of this guide matter more than the third.

A reasonable position

Use AI heavily before the interview and honestly after it. Prepare with it, practise against it, review your transcripts with it. That work compounds and nobody can take the offer back over it.

In the room, the thing that carries you is being able to talk about your own work at depth — which is a preparation problem, not a software problem. If you find yourself needing a tool to answer questions about your own resume, the resume is the thing to fix.

And where you do use live assistance, know which of the three sections above you are in. That is the whole decision.
