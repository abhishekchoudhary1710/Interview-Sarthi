How to answer interview questions in Hinglish

When to switch, when to stay in English, and five worked answers you can steal the structure of.

Updated 29 August 2026 · 7 min read

Every Indian candidate has had this moment. The interview is in English, it is going fine, and then the manager leans back and says, “Accha, ye batao — is project mein sabse bada challenge kya tha?”

And you freeze. Do you answer in Hindi? Switch back to English? Try to translate on the fly and lose half the point?

The short answer: mirror them. If the interviewer switched, you can switch. The longer answer is what this guide is about, because there is a real difference between Hinglish that sounds relaxed and competent, and Hinglish that sounds like you ran out of English.

When Hinglish helps

  • The interviewer switched first. This is the big one. Nine times out of ten they switched to put you at ease or because the point is easier to make in Hindi. Following them reads as comfortable, not sloppy.
  • Indian startups and mid-size companies, especially with the founder or a hiring manager rather than an HR screen.
  • Sales, operations, support, field, and category roles where you will actually work in Hinglish every day. Here it is a demonstration, not a lapse — they want to hear you pitch the way you will pitch to a customer.
  • When the story needs feeling. Frustration, urgency, a tense moment with a client — these often land better in your first language, and interviewers notice when someone stops reciting and starts talking.

When to stay in English

  • The interviewer has stayed in English the whole time. Do not be the one who switches first.
  • MNC panels, global teams, or any call with someone who does not speak Hindi. Obvious, but people slip when they get comfortable.
  • Client-facing roles where the clients are international. The interview is the audition.
  • HR and screening rounds. These are frequently scored against a rubric, sometimes by someone from a different region.
  • Anything written — take-homes, emails, follow-up notes. Hinglish is a spoken register.

The one rule that covers most cases: match the register of the person asking. Switching languages is a social signal, not a linguistic one. Follow, do not lead.

The four habits that separate good Hinglish from bad

1. Keep technical nouns in English

Nobody says “sanchika” for file. Translating jargon into Hindi sounds stilted and makes you harder to follow. Keep connection pool, deployment, latency, funnel, retention exactly as they are and let the grammar around them be Hindi. That is how engineers here actually speak.

2. Keep every number in English

“Forty milliseconds” not “chaalis”. Numbers are the load-bearing part of any good interview answer, and English digits are unambiguous to every listener regardless of what language the sentence is in.

3. Do not narrate in one language and conclude in another

The most common failure is telling the story in Hindi and then trailing off without an English takeaway. Finish the thought. If the answer had a result, state the result clearly in whichever language you started the sentence in.

4. Do not switch mid-clause

“Toh maine usko optimize kiya kyunki latency high thi” is fine — whole clauses, clean joins. “Maine us cheez ko, uh, kaise bolun, jo problem thi…” is not Hinglish, it is hesitation with Hindi filler. The fix is the same as in English: pause instead of filling.

Worked examples

“Is project mein sabse bada challenge kya tha?”

Sabse bada challenge scale ka tha. Launch ke time hum roughly 500 orders a day handle kar rahe the, aur festive season mein wo 8,000 tak chala gaya. Purana sync flow wahan tut raha tha, toh maine order processing ko queue-based kar diya — Redis pe, retries ke saath. Uske baad peak day bhi bina downtime ke nikal gaya.

Why it works: Hindi grammar, English for every technical noun and every number, and it ends on a concrete result.

“Aap job change kyun kar rahe ho?”

Do saal mein maine wahan kaafi seekha, especially backend aur system design pe. Lekin ab team ka scope fix ho gaya hai — same module, same scale. Main aisi jagah dhundh raha hoon jahan ownership zyada ho aur product decisions mein bhi involve ho sakun. Isliye ye role interesting laga.

Why it works: no complaining about the old employer, and the reason points forward at this job rather than away from the last one.

“Tell me about a time you disagreed with your manager.” (asked in English)

Answer in English. They did not switch, so you do not either.

The most useful example in this guide is the one where the answer is “don't”.

“Aapne is role ke liye kya prepare kiya?”

Maine aapke product ko use karke dekha — onboarding flow mein ek jagah friction laga, jahan email verify karne ke baad wapas login screen aa jaati hai. Uske alawa maine aapke tech stack pe padha, especially Kafka ka use, kyunki mere pichle project mein hum RabbitMQ pe the aur trade-offs interesting lage.

Why it works: specific, verifiable, and it gives the interviewer something to ask about next — which is what you want.

“Salary expectation kya hai?”

Market ke hisaab se main 18 to 22 LPA range dekh raha hoon, based on my experience aur is role ki scope. But agar overall package aur growth strong hai, toh main flexible hoon — main sirf number pe decide nahi kar raha.

Numbers in English, a range not a point, and an explicit door left open.

Three mistakes to avoid

  1. Over-correcting into formal Hindi. Shuddh Hindi in a tech interview sounds stranger than Hinglish does. Nobody is grading your Hindi.
  2. Apologising for it. “Sorry, my English is not so good” plants a doubt that was not there. If you can explain a distributed system clearly in any language, your communication is fine. Say the thing; do not apologise for the medium.
  3. Switching because you are stuck. Interviewers can tell the difference between switching for expressiveness and switching to escape. If you do not know the answer, say you do not know and say what you would do to find out — in either language.

How to practise this

Record yourself answering five questions out loud, deliberately mixing the way you would in a real call, and play it back. You are listening for exactly two things: did every answer end on a concrete result, and did you switch mid-clause anywhere. That is a twenty-minute exercise and it is worth more than reading another list of common questions.

Better still, get a friend to run a mock and switch languages on you without warning, so the first time it happens is not in the real interview.
